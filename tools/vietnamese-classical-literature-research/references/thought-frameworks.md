# Hệ tư tưởng làm nền (thought frameworks)

The classical Vietnamese corpus presupposes a shared vocabulary of ideas. When a
text argues from `仁義`, cites `四書五經`, or has a hermit-prefect debate about
`隱逸`, the argument is not decorative — it is the actual logic. This file maps
the frameworks and, more importantly, says what to do with them.

All Han strings here are in `han-nom-keyword-glossary.md` with verification
status. The framework statements are sourced to the corpus pages fetched while
building this skill; where the corpus was silent, the claim is marked as needing
a source rather than asserted.

## The three teachings

`三教` — Confucianism `儒教`, Buddhism `佛教`, Taoism `道教`.

The Vietnamese corpus records two distinct formulae, and the difference is
analytically useful:

- **Tam giáo đồng nguyên** `三教同源` — the three share one root.
- **Tam giáo đồng quy** `三教同歸` — the three arrive at one destination.

A third image appears in the corpus: **Tam giáo tổ sư**, depicting the Buddha
Sakyamuni in the centre with Laozi on the left and Confucius on the right. This
iconography is evidence of how the synthesis was *represented*, not just argued.

The corpus also records that the three teachings completed a process of mutual
permeation and self-adjustment, forming new tendencies. Treat "syncretism" as
something that happened over time and unevenly, not as a static background
assumption.

**What to do:** when a text mixes Buddhist renunciation with Confucian duty, do
not call it inconsistent or call it syncretic and stop. Ask which framework
supplies the *argument* and which supplies the *vocabulary*. That question is
usually the paper.

## Neo-Confucianism: lý học and tâm học

`理學` — the corpus defines it as Confucianism from the two Song dynasties to the
Ming, also called `義理之學` or `道學`. It is rendered **Neo-Confucianism** in
English. Note the corpus's warning: this is distinct from the 20th-century
movement also translated as "New Confucianism" (`當代新儒家`). Conflating them
imports a modern political-intellectual debate into a classical text.

The corpus also records that `理學`, while primarily Confucian, drew on Buddhist
and Taoist thought and is concerned with `性理之學` (nature and principle).

`心學` — a school within Song-Ming Neo-Confucianism. Per the corpus: `陸九淵`
(Lu Jiuyuan, Southern Song) opened the path; it stood against the `程朱理學` of
`程頤` and `朱熹`; the lineage is usually given as 推本於孟子, 濫觴於程顥,
發揚于陆九渊, and 由王陽明集其大成 — hence the name **陆王心学** (Lu-Wang school
of mind).

The two are conventionally paired:

| | 理學 (Cheng-Zhu) | 心學 (Lu-Wang) |
|---|---|---|
| Key figures | 程頤, 朱熹 | 陸九淵, 王陽明 |
| Emphasis | principle inherent in things; investigation of things | principle inherent in the mind |
| Vietnamese reception | dominant in examination orthodoxy | present but not the examination standard |

`朱熹` (Chu Hy, 1130–1200) reinterpreted the Confucian classics through a
`理學` lens; the corpus notes that Song Confucians used the *Yijing*, Buddhism
and Taoism to explain the origin of the cosmos. **His commentaries were the
examination standard**, which is why his readings — not the pre-Song readings —
are what a Vietnamese literate would have known when quoting a classic. This
single fact resolves many apparent misreadings in classical Vietnamese texts:
they are not misreading the classic, they are reading Chu Hy's version.

**What to do:** before claiming a Vietnamese author "misunderstands" Confucius,
check whether the author is following Song commentary. Usually they are, and the
interesting question becomes how they adapt it.

## The examination system

`科舉` — the corpus records it as the recruitment system, with the Vietnamese
equivalents **khoa cử** and **khoa bảng** (`科榜`).

Stages, all verified in the glossary: `鄉試` (hương thí, provincial),
`會試` (hội thí, metropolitan), `殿試` (điện thí, palace). Ranks: `進士`
(tiến sĩ), `狀元` (trạng nguyên), `榜眼` (bảng nhãn), `探花` (thám hoa).
Institutions: `國子監` (Quốc Tử Giám), `翰林院` (Hàn lâm viện), `文廟` (Văn Miếu).

The Vietnamese corpus records **1075** as an early examination date (the first
examination under the Lý), and describes examinations as organised and directed
by the court, with different formats per dynasty.

**Why this matters for literature, not just history:**

1. It explains **who could write** and for whom. The audience of a `誥` or a
   `賦` was examination-trained.
2. It explains **formal habits**. See `八股文` in `genre-and-poetics.md` — the
   eight-legged essay trained a bipartite, balancing mode of argument that
   surfaces in prose that was never an exam answer.
3. It explains a **recurring subject**: examination failure, officialdom,
   retirement, `隱逸`. A large share of classical Vietnamese poetry is written by
   men negotiating this system.
4. It gives you **biographical anchors**. `進士` ranks and examination years are
   datable facts, and often the firmest dating evidence available for an author.

**What to do:** for any author, establish their examination record first. It
dates them, locates them socially, and explains their preoccupations. The
stele records at Văn Miếu and the mộc bản (woodblocks) are physical archives of
exactly this data.

## Recurring ethical vocabulary

| Term | 漢字 | Function in argument |
|---|---|---|
| nhân nghĩa | `仁義` | The core claim of legitimate rule. `平吳大誥` builds its whole case on it ("đại nghĩa thắng hung tàn") |
| trung quân | `忠君` | Loyalty to the ruler; the standard for judging officials |
| hiếu | `孝` | Filial piety; also the frame for mourning texts |
| tiết nghĩa | `節義` | Chastity/integrity under pressure; the corpus records it as a *biographical category* in `安南志略` ("phụ nữ tiết nghĩa") |
| ẩn dật | `隱逸` | Withdrawal; a positive value, not a failure — but often written by men who failed |

Note on `節義`: because it names a category in historical classification, a text
describing a woman as `節義` may be reporting a conventional judgement rather
than describing a person. Recognising the difference between *reporting a
category* and *portraying an individual* is central to reading works like
`南昌女子傳`.

`隱逸` deserves the same caution in reverse: withdrawal poetry by an official who
retired voluntarily reads differently from withdrawal poetry by one who was
passed over, and the biography (examination record, offices held) tells you
which you have.

## The two governing doctrines of writing

`文以載道` — literature carries the Way. `詩言志` — poetry expresses the intent.

Both were confirmed in the corpus, and they appear together in Vietnamese
scholarship as a paired reading frame: the fetched Vietnamese corpus quotes a
scholar placing an author within `văn dĩ tải đạo` and `thi ngôn chí`, with the
themes `vệ đạo, bảo dân, trung quân, ái quốc` running through the reading.

These two are not the same claim and pulling them apart is productive:

- `文以載道` makes writing instrumental — its value is the doctrine it transmits.
  It licenses didacticism and makes aesthetic pleasure suspect.
- `詩言志` makes poetry expressive — its value is the sincerity of the intent.
  It licenses personal voice.

A great deal of classical Vietnamese writing lives in the tension between them:
texts that must carry the Way but are read for their voice. **When a work seems
to fail at one, check whether it was aiming at the other.**

`詩言志` traces to the *Thượng thư* tradition — the Chinese corpus quotes it in
the 夔 passage: `詩言志，歌永言，聲依永，律和聲`. It also appears as the title of
a modern study, 朱自清's `詩言志辨`, which is a useful secondary entry point.

##天人合一 and cosmology

`天人合一` — the unity of heaven and human. Verified in the glossary. Related:
`陰陽`, `五行`.

Function in the corpus: this is the framework behind **omens, portents, and the
legitimacy of rule**. When a text records an eclipse, a strange beast, or a
dream and then a political event, the connection is not superstition decorating
history — under this framework the correlation *is* the argument.

It also explains the machinery of `志怪` and `傳奇`: the underworld trial in
`傘圓祠判事錄` is not fantasy escape but a moral-juridical cosmology working as
designed — the cosmic order has a legal procedure, and a righteous man can win
by following it.

**What to do:** when a text deploys the marvellous, ask what the cosmology
permits. Then ask whether the author uses it to endorse or to satirise the
worldly order. `傳奇漫錄` does both, sometimes in one story.

## Using frameworks without flattening the text

Three failure modes this file exists to prevent:

1. **Labelling instead of reading.** Writing "the poem reflects Confucian
   thought" is not analysis. Say which concept does what work in which line.
2. **Importing the Chinese debate.** Song-Ming philosophical controversies have
   their own history. A Vietnamese author adapting `理學` in the 15th century is
   doing something different from a Ming author in the 16th. Date the reception,
   don't just name the school.
3. **Assuming the author's own belief.** Texts perform orthodoxy for an
   examination-trained audience. A conventional `忠君` statement is evidence about
   the genre's expectations, not necessarily about the author. Biography and
   context decide.

The productive question is almost always: **which framework is doing the arguing
here, and what does the author gain by using it rather than another?**
